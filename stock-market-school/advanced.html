01 · Valuation

Price is what you pay. Value is what you get.

The core ratioP/E — price to earningsA stock at $100 earning $5 per share trades at P/E 20: you pay 20 years of today's profit. Flip it: 5/100 = a 5% earnings yield — compare that to what bonds pay.
The catchGrowth is already in the priceA high P/E isn't "expensive" and a low one isn't "cheap" — it's the market's forecast. Nvidia at P/E 60 means the crowd expects earnings to multiply. The question is never "is it good?" but "is it better than what's priced in?"
The consequenceGreat company ≠ great stockCisco was a phenomenal business in 2000. Buyers at the peak waited ~20 years to break even — the price already assumed perfection.
02 · Financial statements

Three documents tell the whole story

The movieIncome statementRevenue → costs → net income, over a quarter or year. Watch the margin (profit ÷ revenue): a rising margin means pricing power.
The snapshotBalance sheetWhat the company owns vs owes right now. Heavy debt isn't fatal — until interest rates rise. Cash-rich companies survive winters.
The lie detectorCash flow statementAccounting profit can be massaged; cash entering the bank cannot. Profitable on paper + burning cash = red flag. Free cash flow is the number pros trust.

First habit of serious investors: read the cash flow statement before the press release.

03 · Interest rates

Rates are gravity for every asset price

A stock is a claim on future profits — and future money must be discounted to today's value. The discount rate starts from the central-bank rate. When rates rise, tomorrow's shekel is worth less today, so long-duration assets (growth stocks) fall hardest. When rates fall, the same cash flows are suddenly worth more.

EnvironmentBonds payWhat it does to stocks
Zero rates (2010s)~0–2%No alternative → money floods into equities, valuations stretch
Rising rates (2022)4–5%"Safe 5%" competes with stocks → P/E compresses, growth −30%+
Cutting cyclefallingDiscount rates drop → valuations re-expand

You don't need to predict the Fed. You need to not be surprised that rates move markets.

04 · Index mechanics

Why the index is so hard to beat

  • Market-cap weighting: the S&P 500 isn't 500 equal bets — the biggest companies get the biggest weight. Your index money automatically rides the winners as they grow.
  • Self-cleansing: failures shrink and drop out; rising stars get added. The index fires losers and hires winners — with zero management fee for the decision.
  • Skewness: most stocks are mediocre; a tiny handful (4% historically) create nearly all net wealth. Stock-pickers must find those needles — the index simply owns the whole haystack.
  • The evidence: over 15-year windows, roughly 9 out of 10 professional active funds underperform their benchmark after fees (SPIVA data).

Beating the index isn't impossible. It's just statistically arrogant to assume you're the exception.

05 · Diversification math

The only free lunch in finance

Diversification isn't "owning many things" — it's owning things that don't move together (correlation < 1). Combine imperfectly-correlated assets and portfolio volatility drops below the average of its parts, while expected return stays the average. Less volatility also means less volatility drag: lose 50%, and you need +100% just to get back to zero.

DropGain needed to recover
−10%+11%
−30%+43%
−50%+100%
−80%+400%

Deep losses are asymmetric. The first job of a portfolio is to make −50% impossible.

06 · Behavioral finance

The most expensive organ in investing is the amygdala

Loss aversionLosses hurt ~2× more than gains feel goodThat asymmetry makes people sell bottoms and avoid buying after crashes — the exact opposite of profitable behavior.
The behavior gapInvestors earn less than their own fundsStudies (DALBAR, Morningstar) find the average investor underperforms the very funds they hold by ~1.5–2%/yr — purely from ill-timed entries and exits.
Recency & FOMO"This time it keeps going up"Whatever rose recently feels safe (it isn't) and whatever crashed feels dangerous (that's often the discount). Momentum of feelings ≠ momentum of value.

Write rules when calm; execute them when scared. Never invert that order.

07 · Portfolio construction

Core–satellite: discipline at the center, curiosity at the edge

85–95% · CoreBroad index funds, automated monthlyGlobal or S&P 500 tracker, lowest fees you can find, auto-invested. Boring by design — this is the wealth engine.
5–15% · SatelliteYour active ideas, ring-fencedSingle stocks, themes, crypto — capped so a total wipeout is survivable. Track its performance honestly against the core. Most people learn the core wins.
DeploymentLump sum vs DCAStatistically a lump sum beats drip-feeding ~2 times out of 3 (markets rise more often than not). But if a crash the day after would break you emotionally — spreading over months is the psychologically correct choice.
08 · Costs & taxes (Israeli investor)

The only two things you fully control

  • Capital gains tax: 25% on real (inflation-adjusted) gains, charged when you sell. No sale — no tax event: long holding = free tax deferral, compounding works on the government's share too.
  • Irish-domiciled accumulating ETFs reinvest dividends without a taxable distribution and cut US dividend withholding from 30% to 15% — structurally efficient for long-term Israeli holders.
  • Management fees compound against you: 0.07% vs 0.8% TER on the same index ≈ hundreds of thousands of shekels over an investing lifetime. Fee comparison is the highest-paid hour of work in finance.
  • Tax-advantaged wrappers first: Keren Hishtalmut (tax-free at eligibility) and Kupat Gemel LeHashkaa (annuity route exempt at 60) beat a plain brokerage account for the same index exposure.

The advanced golden rules

What separates pros from tourists

  • Think in ranges, not points. Any forecast with one number is marketing; real planning uses the Monte Carlo fan.
  • Expected return is paid for enduring drawdowns. If you can't sit through −30%, you don't own stocks — they own you.
  • Process over outcome. A good decision can lose money and a reckless one can win. Grade the process; luck evens out, discipline compounds.
  • Costs, taxes, behavior — in that order of controllability. Alpha is a maybe; a saved fee is a certainty.
  • Position size is risk management. The satellite stays capped no matter how convinced you are. Especially then.
